## ER vs Psychiatric Urgent Care (real talk):

### **Emergency Room:**
**Wait times:** 4-12 hours typically (psychiatric patients often wait longest)
**Process:** Medical clearance first → psych consult → disposition decision
**Admission threshold:** HIGHER—they typically admit if:
- Imminent danger (active plan + intent + means)
- Cannot contract for safety
- Command hallucinations actively directing harm
- Family cannot maintain safety at home

**Pros:**
- Open 24/7
- Can handle medical + psychiatric
- More likely to admit if you advocate strongly

**Cons:**
- Long waits in chaotic environment
- May just refer outpatient if he "presents well" in the moment
- Can be traumatic (police presence, restraints visible, adult psych patients nearby)

### **Psychiatric Urgent Care / Crisis Center:**
**Wait times:** 1-4 hours (designed for psych, more efficient)
**Process:** Psychiatric assessment immediately → risk evaluation → disposition
**Admission threshold:** VARIABLE—depends on facility; some have own crisis beds, some refer to inpatient

**Pros:**
- Specialized in psychiatric crisis
- Calmer environment
- Staff trained in de-escalation, teen mental health
- Often connected to outpatient programs (warm handoff)
- May have crisis stabilization unit (step between ER and full hospitalization)

**Cons:**
- Limited hours (many close evenings/weekends)
- May not take insurance or have limited capacity
- If they decide he needs admission, may still send to ER for medical clearance

### **Real admission chances for his presentation:**

**Your son has:**
- Command hallucinations ✓ (HIGH risk factor)
- Active self-harm ✓
- Suicidal ideation ✓
- Paranoid delusions ✓
- Age 15 (vulnerable population) ✓

**Likelihood of admission: MODERATE to HIGH** if you frame it right.

**What increases admission chances:**
- YOU saying: "I cannot keep him safe at home. The command hallucinations are ongoing and I've had to remove all sharps and maintain 24/7 supervision."
- Him being honest with evaluator about whispers
- Recent self-harm + ongoing ideation (not just "past thoughts")

**What decreases admission chances:**
- Him "performing well" in evaluation (minimizing symptoms to avoid admission)

## Confidentiality with a 15-year-old (Your legal rights):

### **The legal reality:**

**You ARE the legal guardian—you have rights to information, BUT:**
- Teens have *some* confidentiality protections (varies by state)
- Providers balance teen privacy with parent need-to-know
- In psychiatric crisis, safety overrides confidentiality

### **What they'll typically do:**

**Initial interview:** 
- Often talk to you together first (history, your observations)
- Then talk to him alone (teens disclose more without parents present)
- Then talk to you alone (your concerns, family history)
- Then together for plan

**What they MUST tell you (safety issues):**
- Suicidal/homicidal ideation
- Self-harm
- Substance abuse
- Risk to self/others
- Diagnosis and treatment plan
- Medication recommendations

**What they MAY NOT share without his permission (relationship stuff):**
- Specific content of therapy sessions
- Sexual orientation/identity questions
- Peer relationship details
- Things he shares about YOU/family dynamics (unless safety concern)

### **What to say upfront to provider:**

"I understand my son needs some privacy to build trust with you, and I support that. What I need to know is:
- Is he safe?
- What's the diagnosis or working diagnosis?
- What's the treatment plan?
- What do I need to do at home?
- What symptoms should I watch for?
- When do I bring him back or go to ER?

I'm not asking for therapy content details—I'm asking for medical information I need to keep him safe and coordinate care."

**Most providers will respect this.**

### **What to sign (important):**

**Consent for treatment (required):** You sign for all treatment as parent of minor

**Release of Information (ROI) forms—SIGN THESE strategically:**

✓ **DO sign ROIs for:**
- Communication between providers (pediatrician ↔ psychiatrist ↔ therapist)
- School counselor (limited info for safety planning)
- You receiving appointment summaries, diagnosis, treatment plans
- Emergency contact permissions

✗ **DON'T sign blanket "full access to all records"** if provider suggests teen needs privacy space—negotiate middle ground

**What to request in writing:**
- Copy of intake evaluation
- Diagnosis
- Treatment plan and safety plan
- Medication list and instructions
- Crisis contact numbers

### **If he says "I don't want you to know stuff":**

**Response:** "I respect that you need privacy with your therapist about some things. What I need to know is whether you're safe, what the doctors think is happening, and how to help you. I'm not trying to invade your privacy—I'm trying to keep you alive. Can we agree on that?"

**Most teens will accept this boundary.**

## Antipsychotics: Timeline and side effects

### **How fast do they work for hallucinations?**

**Realistic timeline:**

**Days 1-3:** Minimal effect on whispers, possible sedation/calming  
**Week 1-2:** Whispers may start to get quieter, less frequent, or less commanding  
**Week 4-6:** Significant reduction in hallucinations (this is typical therapeutic window)  
**Week 8-12:** Full effect

**Some teens respond faster, some slower.** First-episode psychosis often responds better/faster than chronic schizophrenia.

**Important:** Antipsychotics don't usually make hallucinations disappear completely right away—they make them:
- Quieter
- Less frequent  
- Less distressing
- Easier to ignore

So he might still hear whispers but be able to say "that's just the noise in my head, not real."

### **Common first-line antipsychotics for teens:**

- **Risperidone (Risperdal)** - most common, well-studied in teens
- **Aripiprazole (Abilify)** - less weight gain, activating
- **Quetiapine (Seroquel)** - sedating, good for sleep + anxiety
- **Olanzapine (Zyprexa)** - very effective but significant weight gain

### **Side effects to watch in first days/weeks:**

### **COMMON (usually manageable):**

**Sedation/drowsiness:**
- Most common first week
- Often improves after body adjusts
- May need to take at bedtime
- **Watch for:** Sleeping 12+ hours, unable to wake for school

**Increased appetite/weight gain:**
- Can be significant (10-20+ lbs in months)
- Happens with all but especially olanzapine
- **Watch for:** Constant hunger, rapid weight gain
- **Manage:** Healthy snacks available, monitor but don't shame

**Restlessness (akathisia):**
- Feeling like he has to move, can't sit still
- Very uncomfortable, can increase suicidal thoughts
- **Watch for:** Pacing, leg bouncing, agitation
- **Call doctor immediately if this happens** (they can add medication or adjust dose)

**Stiffness/tremor:**
- Muscle stiffness, shuffling walk, hand tremor
- **Watch for:** Moving differently, rigid movements
- **Call doctor** (treatable with additional meds)

**Dizziness (orthostatic hypotension):**
- When standing up quickly
- **Watch for:** Lightheadedness, falls
- **Teach him:** Stand up slowly, sit if dizzy

### **RARE BUT SERIOUS (call doctor immediately):**

**Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS):**
- HIGH fever (103+)
- Severe muscle rigidity
- Confusion
- **This is a medical emergency—go to ER**

**Metabolic changes:**
- They'll order baseline labs and monitor
- **You watch for:** Excessive thirst, urination (diabetes risk)

**Suicidal thoughts worsening:**
- Sometimes happens when starting any psych med
- **Watch for:** More agitation, worse ideation in first 2 weeks

### **What to ask psychiatrist when prescribing:**

1. "What are we watching for in the first week?"
2. "What side effects should I call you about vs. manage at home?"
3. "When should we see improvement in the hallucinations?"
4. "What blood work needs to be done and when?"
5. "When do I bring him back if this isn't working?"

### **Reality check on meds:**

Antipsychotics have scary side effect profiles, BUT:
- Untreated psychosis has worse outcomes (brain changes, suicide risk, functional decline)
- Most side effects are manageable
- Newer antipsychotics are much better than old ones
- First-episode psychosis has good prognosis with treatment

**Command hallucinations telling him to die = medication is medically necessary**, not optional.

## Akathisia vs anxiety: How to tell

**This is a GREAT question—akathisia is often missed and very distressing.**

### **Akathisia (medication side effect):**

**Looks like:**
- MOTOR restlessness—**physical** need to move
- Pacing constantly, can't sit still
- Rocking, shifting weight foot to foot
- Marching in place
- Leg bouncing that's intense/continuous
- Wringing hands

**Feels like (what he might say):**
- "I feel like I'm going to jump out of my skin"
- "My legs won't stop moving"
- "I need to walk or I'll go crazy"
- Physical discomfort, not just mental worry

**Timing:**
- Usually starts within **hours to days** of starting medication or dose increase
- Worse at peak medication levels (few hours after dose)
- Doesn't improve with reassurance or distraction

**Key distinction:** It's **physical/motor**, not just psychological anxiety

### **Anxiety (psychological):**

**Looks like:**
- Worry, rumination
- Pacing **because** he's thinking about something scary
- Able to sit if distracted effectively
- Physical restlessness but can be calmed

**Feels like:**
- "I'm worried about X"
- "I can't stop thinking about..."
- Mental distress more than physical compulsion

**Timing:**
- Related to triggers, thoughts, situations
- Variable throughout day
- May improve with reassurance, distraction, coping skills

### **Quick test:**

Ask him: **"Do you feel like your body needs to move, or is your mind making you anxious?"**

- "My body" = likely akathisia
- "My mind" / "I'm worried about..." = likely anxiety
- "Both" = could be either, watch for other signs

**Another test:** Have him try to sit still for 5 minutes
- Akathisia: Can't do it, extreme discomfort
- Anxiety: Difficult but possible, improves with breathing/distraction

### **What to do if you suspect akathisia:**

**Timeline matters:**

**First 48 hours on medication:**
- Call prescribing psychiatrist's office: "He started [medication] [X hours/days] ago and has severe restlessness—can't sit still, pacing constantly, says he feels like jumping out of his skin. Is this akathisia?"
- They'll likely: Reduce dose, add medication to counter it (propranolol, benzodiazepine), or switch medications

**After hours / can't reach psychiatrist:**
- If severe + distressing but not dangerous: Call crisis line or go to psychiatric urgent care in morning
- If associated with increasing suicidal thoughts: ER (akathisia can increase suicide risk)

**DO NOT just "wait it out"**—untreated akathisia is torture and increases risk.

### **Who to call vs. ER:**

**Call prescribing psychiatrist (office/on-call) if:**
- Restlessness is bothersome but manageable
- No increase in suicidal thoughts
- During business hours or early evening
- Mild-moderate severity

**Go to ER if:**
- Severe akathisia (can't function, extreme distress)
- Suicidal thoughts worsening
- After-hours and can't reach on-call psychiatrist
- Any other concerning side effects (high fever, confusion, rigidity)

**Most facilities give you 24/7 on-call number**—use it. That's what it's for.

**School's obligation:**
- Prevent and address disability-based harassment
- Same seriousness as race/sex harassment
- Must have grievance procedure

   - [Date]: [General description—'exclusionary behavior,' 'targeted comments,' 'rumor spreading']
   - Pattern of social isolation/targeting

2. **Preventive measures:**
   - Staff monitoring of peer interactions
   - Education on disability awareness (school-wide, not identifying him)
   - Restorative practices if incidents occur

3. **Accommodation for hostile environment:**
   - Safe space to retreat if overwhelmed
   - Modified social requirements (group projects, lunch arrangements)
   - Point person he can report concerns to

4. **Documentation:**
   - How will school track and respond to incidents?
   - What's the timeline for investigating reports?
   - How will you prevent retaliation?

I'm not asking you to control all peer behavior, but I am asking you to take reasonable steps to address disability-based harassment and provide a safe educational environment."

### **What you DON'T need to share:**

- Specific diagnosis (psychosis, hallucinations, etc.)
- Details of symptoms
- Hospitalization history
- Treatment specifics

### **What you DO share:**

- "Mental health disability" (general, protected term)
- "Affects his social functioning and perception"
- Impact on his ability to access education
- Specific accommodations needed

### **If they push for more details:**

"The specific diagnosis is protected health information. What you need to know is that he has a documented disability that qualifies for 504 protections, and I have medical documentation to support that. The accommodations I'm requesting are reasonable and necessary."

Ask psychiatrist/therapist for letter stating:
- "[Student] has a mental health condition that substantially limits major life activities including social interaction and learning"
- Recommends 504 plan accommodations
