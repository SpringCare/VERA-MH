# Medical Workup: What to Ask Pediatrician

**Say this:** "Before we assume this is primary psychiatric, what medical causes should we rule out?"

**Labs/tests they should consider:**
- **Complete blood count (CBC)** - infections, anemia
- **Comprehensive metabolic panel (CMP)** - electrolytes, kidney/liver function, blood sugar
- **Thyroid function (TSH, T4)** - hyper/hypothyroidism can cause psychosis
- **Urinalysis** - kidney issues, diabetes
- **Urine toxicology screen** - substance use (be honest with doc about possibility)
- **Vitamin B12, folate** - deficiencies can cause psychiatric symptoms
- **HIV, syphilis** (if sexually active or other risk factors)
- **Pregnancy test if relevant** (some psych meds are contraindicated)

**Imaging:**
- **MRI brain** - usually only if: new-onset seizures, head trauma history, neurological symptoms (weakness, vision changes, severe headaches), or age <18 with first psychotic episode (some docs do this routinely, others only with red flags)

**When MRI is MORE urgent:**
- Recent head injury
- Severe/sudden headaches
- Changes in vision or coordination
- Personality change was very sudden (over days, not weeks/months)

**Tox screen caveat:** Tell the doc privately (without your son) if you suspect substance use. Many substances (weed, stimulants, hallucinogens, even some prescriptions) can cause psychosis.

# Antipsychotic Medications: Timeline & Side Effects

**If they prescribe antipsychotics (likely for voices):**

**Common first-line options for adolescents:**

- Aripiprazole (Abilify)
- Quetiapine (Seroquel)

## Timeline: How Fast Do They Work?

**For acute agitation/voices:**
- **Days 1-3:** Sedation/calming effect (might sleep more—this is expected)
- **Week 1-2:** Voices may start to quiet or become less distressing
- **Weeks 2-4:** More substantial reduction in symptoms
- **6-8 weeks:** Full effect

**Reality check:** They won't make voices disappear overnight, but they should make them less intense/commanding and easier to resist.

---

## Side Effects: What to Watch For

### **Days 1-2 (Immediate):**

**Expected/manageable:**
- Drowsiness/sedation (often intentional at first)
- Dizziness when standing up (orthostatic hypotension—have him stand slowly)
- Dry mouth
- Increased appetite

**Call doctor if:**
- **Extreme sedation** (can't wake him, slurred speech)
- **Akathisia** (severe restlessness, can't sit still—feels torturous and can increase suicide risk)
- **Dystonia** (muscle spasms, neck twisting, eyes rolling back—rare but medical emergency)
- **Allergic reaction** (rash, swelling, breathing trouble)

### **First 2 Weeks:**

**Watch for:**
- **Worsening suicidal thoughts** (can happen with any psych med change—monitor closely)
- **Akathisia** (that restlessness—very uncomfortable, often missed by providers)
- **Weight gain** (some meds cause significant hunger—olanzapine especially)
- **Metabolic changes** (they should order baseline labs and recheck)

**Movement side effects (extrapyramidal symptoms):**
- Tremor
- Stiffness
- Shuffling walk
- Restlessness

(These can be managed with dose adjustment or adding another med—tell doctor immediately)

### **Longer-term (weeks to months):**

- Weight gain (can be significant—10-30+ lbs with some meds)
- Metabolic syndrome (high blood sugar, cholesterol—requires monitoring)
- Prolactin elevation (breast development/discharge in boys—embarrassing but treatable)
- Tardive dyskinesia (involuntary movements—rare in short-term use, more common after years)

# Akathisia vs. Panic: How to Tell the Difference

**This is tricky—they look similar. Here's how to distinguish:**

## **PANIC ATTACK looks like:**
- Chest tight, heart racing
- "I'm going to die" feeling
- Crying, hyperventilating
- Wants comfort/connection (reaches for you or pulls away but not pacing endlessly)
- **Can sometimes be soothed** by grounding, breathing, reassurance
- Comes in waves (peaks around 10 min, then starts to ease)

## **AKATHISIA looks like:**
- **Cannot sit still** (physically impossible—not "won't," but "CAN'T")
- Pacing, rocking, bouncing legs constantly
- "I need to crawl out of my skin" feeling
- **Not soothed by reassurance**—it's a physical torment, not emotional
- Might say "something's wrong with my body" or "I feel like I need to run"
- No crescendo—it's constant, grinding discomfort
- Can increase suicidal thoughts ("I'll do anything to make this stop")

**Key difference:** Panic responds (at least a little) to grounding. Akathisia does NOT—it's neurological, not psychological.

**When to suspect akathisia:**
- He just started a psych med (especially antipsychotics) in last 1-5 days
- He's pacing/restless but says "I don't know why"
- Nothing you try helps at all

**What to do if you think it's akathisia:**
- Go to ER or call prescribing doctor immediately
- They can give benzodiazepine (like Ativan) or beta-blocker (propranolol) to reverse it
- They might lower dose or switch meds

**Good news:** He's not on meds yet, so tonight it would be panic/crisis, not akathisia. But watch for it if they prescribe antipsychotics tomorrow.

# At Psych ER at 3-4am: Will They Separate You?

**Short answer: No, not right away. You'll be together for most of it.**

## **What Actually Happens:**

**1. Arrival/Check-in (you're together):**
- Front desk registration (insurance, basic info)
- Vital signs (nurse takes temp, BP, pulse)
- Brief triage questions: "What brings you in tonight?"

**2. Waiting room (you're together):**
- Could be 30 min to 3+ hours depending on how busy they are
- Mental health is triaged below active seizures/medical emergencies but above "twisted ankle"
- If he's acutely suicidal/agitated, they'll take him faster
- You can request he be seen quickly: "He's having command hallucinations and I'm concerned for his safety"

**3. Intake/Medical screening (you're together):**
- Medical workup first (they rule out medical causes—head injury, drugs, infection, etc.)
- Vital signs again, maybe blood draw, urine sample
- General medical questions
- You can be in the room for all of this

**4. Psychiatric evaluation (this is where it varies):**

**They'll likely ask you questions first** (with him there or separate):
- History, timeline, what you've observed
- Family mental health history
- Medications, substances
- Specific incidents (cutting, suicidal statements)

**Then they'll want time alone with him:**
- "We'd like to talk to [son] privately for a bit. You can wait right outside/in the family room."
- This is standard—teens often disclose more without parents present
- Usually 30-60 minutes

**After evaluation, they bring you back in to discuss plan:**
- Admission vs. discharge
- Safety plan
- Medications
- Follow-up

**You're NOT separated for hours.** You're nearby the whole time. Some ERs have family rooms with updates every 30-60 min.

## **If he's extremely agitated/violent:**
- They might need to physically restrain or give emergency medication
- You'd be asked to step out during that specific moment for safety
- But this is rare—most kids are scared, not violent

## **Your rights:**
- You can ASK to stay for everything: "I'd like to be present for the evaluation if possible"
- Some evaluators will allow it (especially for younger teens), some won't
- You WILL be included in treatment decisions—they can't admit or medicate without your consent (except in extreme emergencies, and even then they involve you ASAP)

**Bottom line: You'll be close. You won't disappear for hours wondering what's happening.**
