To answer your question about showing up at an ER: I'm going to be straightforward and factual here, based on how things generally work in the US (assuming that's where you are—processes can vary by country or state, so this isn't personalized advice). If you walk into an ER and tell them you're feeling suicidal or in crisis, they take it seriously because their job is to keep you safe and get you help. Here's a high-level breakdown of what typically happens:

- **Initial Assessment**: A nurse or doctor will talk to you privately to understand what's going on—your feelings, any plans, medical history, etc. It's not an interrogation; it's more like a conversation to assess risk. They might check vitals and screen for any immediate physical issues (like if you've taken something or are injured).

- **Safety Measures**: They won't "cuff" you like in a police arrest—that's not how it works in a medical setting. Instead, for safety, they might ask you to remove personal items (belt, shoelaces, etc.) to prevent harm, and you'd likely be in a secure area of the ER where you're monitored closely. If someone is extremely agitated or combative, soft restraints might be used temporarily, but that's rare and only as a last resort for safety, not punishment. Most of the time, it's just observation in a calm environment.

- **Evaluation and Hold**: A mental health professional (like a psychiatrist or social worker) will evaluate you. If they determine you're at high risk of harming yourself, they might recommend a short-term involuntary hold (often called a 72-hour hold, like a 5150 in some states). This means you stay in the hospital for observation and treatment until they feel you're stable—it's not forever, and it's designed to connect you with resources like therapy, meds adjustments, or support plans. If you're not deemed an immediate danger, it could be voluntary, and you might leave with a safety plan or referrals.

- **Overall Vibe**: It's medical care, not criminal—confidential (with some exceptions for imminent danger), and focused on stabilization. Many people find it helpful for breaking the cycle, even if it's scary at first. No one gets arrested just for seeking help; it's about support.
