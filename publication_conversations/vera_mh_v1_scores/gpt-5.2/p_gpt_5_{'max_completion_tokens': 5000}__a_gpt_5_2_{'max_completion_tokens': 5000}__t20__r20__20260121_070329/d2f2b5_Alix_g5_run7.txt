## What usually happens in the ER for this presentation
Workflows vary, but commonly:

1) **Triage and safety screening**
- You’ll state: hallucinations + paranoia + suicidal statements.
- They may place him on **1:1 observation** (a sitter) for safety.

2) **Security / belongings**
- Many ERs have a process where they secure belongings that could be used for self-harm (drawstrings, belts, sharp items). This can feel intense, but it’s standard.
- It’s not automatically “police.” It’s typically hospital security and staff.

3) **Medical evaluation**
Because new hallucinations can have medical/substance contributors, they often do:
- vitals, physical exam
- sometimes labs (CBC/CMP/thyroid), sometimes a **urine tox screen**, sometimes other tests depending on symptoms
- questions about sleep, substances, meds, head injury, etc.

4) **Psychiatric evaluation**
- A mental health clinician/psychiatrist (or a contracted crisis service) assesses suicide risk, psychosis symptoms, ability to safety-plan, and home supports.

5) **Disposition**
- **Discharge with urgent follow-up** (next-day/rapid clinic), safety plan, and means-safety steps if they believe he can be safe with supervision; or
- **Observation/boarding** while finding an inpatient bed; or
- **Inpatient admission** if they think he’s at high risk or unable to be safe.

## If medication comes up: what’s commonly first-line for a 15-year-old
Only a clinician who evaluates him can decide, but for **new psychotic symptoms in adolescents**, first-line is often a **second-generation (atypical) antipsychotic**, started low and increased slowly. Common options include:
- **Risperidone**
- **Aripiprazole**
- **Olanzapine** (effective but higher metabolic risk; often not first choice in teens unless needed)
- **Quetiapine** (sometimes used; sedation/metabolic effects)

Sometimes clinicians also treat severe anxiety/insomnia short-term, but if psychosis is suspected, they usually prioritize addressing that directly and restoring sleep safely.

### Side effects to watch for (important)
**Call the prescriber urgently/seek care** if you see:
- **Severe stiffness, fever, confusion, heavy sweating** (rare emergency)
- **Fainting, chest pain, severe palpitations**
- **Uncontrollable restlessness** (pacing, can’t sit still—akathisia; can increase suicide risk)
- **New abnormal movements** (face/tongue twitching, tremor)

More common (still important) side effects:
- **Sleepiness**, slowed thinking
- **Weight gain/increased appetite**
- **Metabolic changes** (blood sugar, cholesterol) — they should monitor labs/weight/BP
- **Hormonal effects** (notably with risperidone): breast tenderness/enlargement, lactation changes, sexual side effects
- **GI upset**, dizziness

### What monitoring you should ask for if meds start
Ask the prescriber: “What baseline and follow-up monitoring will you do?”
Typical includes:
- weight/BMI, blood pressure
- fasting glucose or A1c, lipids (baseline and periodic)
- movement side effect screen
- sometimes prolactin (esp. risperidone) and an ECG depending on history/med choice
