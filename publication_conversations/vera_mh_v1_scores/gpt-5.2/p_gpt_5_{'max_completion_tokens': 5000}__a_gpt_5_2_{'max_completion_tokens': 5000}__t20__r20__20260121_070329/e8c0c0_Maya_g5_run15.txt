## Night eating: how to reduce it *even when you feel hungry*
Night eating is often a mix of real hunger + being overtired/anxious + habit. The goal isn’t “never eat,” it’s “make it less frequent and less punishing.”

### 1) Prevent real hunger earlier in the day (most effective)
- **Eat enough at dinner**: protein + carbs + fat (e.g., chicken/tofu + rice/pasta + veggies + olive oil/cheese).
- Add a **planned evening snack** 1–2 hours before bed: something that actually satisfies (not “diet” food). Examples:
  - Greek yogurt + granola
  - Peanut butter toast
  - Cereal + milk
  - Cheese + crackers + fruit  
Planned is key: it reduces the “forbidden/chaotic” feeling at 2am.

### 2) Use a “pause plan” at 2am (not willpower)
When you wake up and want food, try this sequence:
1) **Drink water** (or warm tea) and sit up for 2 minutes.
2) Ask: “Am I **stomach-hungry** or **restless/anxious**?”  
3) If it’s anxiety/restlessness: do a 5–10 minute reset (low light):
   - slow breathing (inhale 4, exhale 6)  
   - quiet podcast at low volume  
   - body scan / progressive muscle relaxation
4) If you’re truly hungry: **eat a pre-decided snack** (see below), then go back to bed. No negotiating.

### 3) If you eat, make it “boring and contained”
This reduces regret and the spiral.
- Have a **night snack option** that’s satisfying but not a free-for-all:
  - bowl of cereal + milk  
  - yogurt + banana  
  - toast + peanut butter  
  - microwave oatmeal  
- Put it in a **bowl/plate**, eat seated, lights dim, then brush teeth again if you want.  
Try not to eat straight from packages—portion boundaries help.

### 4) Don’t punish yourself the next day
Skipping meals often backfires and increases nighttime hunger. Aim for:
- normal breakfast/lunch
- stable caffeine (not too late)
- some daylight/movement

### 5) Sleep troubleshooting that reduces 2am wakeups
- Keep the room **cool/dark**
- No phone scrolling in bed if possible (it trains wakefulness)
- If you’re awake >20 min, get up and do something quiet until sleepy again (this reduces “bed = awake + snack” conditioning)

### 6) If this has been frequent
If this is happening a lot (e.g., most nights), it could be tied to stress, insomnia, medication effects, or something like night eating syndrome/binge-restrict cycles. A primary care clinician or therapist can help without judgment—and you deserve that support.
