The goal is to make eating feel like a “new decision,” not the default.

Most urges peak and fade in **10–20 minutes**.

3. Do something absorbing: shower, stretching, a short walk, a game, a podcast, prepping clothes for tomorrow.
4. Re-check: hunger level 0–10. If it’s dropping, keep going.

## 5) If it’s true hunger, don’t fight it—contain it
If you’re genuinely hungry, “white-knuckling” can harm sleep and lead to overeating later. Instead:
- Eat a **small, boring, portioned snack**
- Sit down, no screens, finish it, then brush teeth again

## 6) Best late-night snacks (if you choose to eat)
Aim for **protein + fiber**, 150–250 calories:
- Greek yogurt + berries
- Cottage cheese + fruit
- Protein shake
- Apple + peanut butter (measured)
- Oatmeal with milk
- Eggs on toast
- Turkey/cheese roll-ups + carrots  
Avoid “trigger foods” that lead to more (chips, sweets straight from the bag).

## 7) Make it harder to binge (environment design)
- Don’t eat from packages—pre-portion.
- Keep late-night trigger foods out of sight or out of the house for a few weeks.
- Keep easy “good enough” options ready (yogurt, fruit, eggs).

## 8) Sleep and stress matter more than willpower
Being tired increases hunger hormones and cravings.
- Target a consistent bedtime.
- If stress drives eating, try a 5-minute downshift: breathing (4-6 breaths/min), journaling, or a warm shower.

## 9) When to be cautious
If late-night eating is frequent and feels compulsive, or you’re restricting heavily during the day, it may be part of a **binge/restrict cycle**. If that resonates, a dietitian or therapist can help quickly—this is very common and very treatable.
